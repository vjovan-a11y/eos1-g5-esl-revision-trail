# Exam-style guide: Grade 5 ESL (Cambridge Primary ESL Stage 6), End of Semester 1

This guide says HOW the Grade 5 ESL EOS1 exam asks questions. It never says WHAT it asks. Everything you write
must be new: new texts, new talks, new people, new places, new situations.

## Level and language
- Learners: Vietnamese children aged 10 to 11, Cambridge Primary ESL Stage 6. Level about CEFR A2+ to B1 (Lexile 700L to 900L).
- British English spelling and vocabulary (colour, favourite, centre, practise (verb), programme, mum, football, holiday, autumn, timetable, film).
- Short, clear sentences. Every text must be easy to picture. Friendly, school-age situations. No brands, no real celebrities, no real living people.
- Names: use a mix of international names (e.g. Leo, Maya, Omar, Freya, Kenji, Priya, Sami, Isla, Tariq, Elena) and some Vietnamese names (e.g. Minh, Lan, Huy, Mai, An, Bao). Do not reuse a name inside the same stop for different people.
- Thinking levels (Webb DOK). Level 1 = find or recognise something said directly. Level 2 = understand a paraphrase, infer, compare, decide what someone means or thinks. Level 3 is NOT used in these papers. In every set of 10 test items at least 3 must be Level 1 and at least 3 Level 2 (we use 5 + 5).

## The four EOS1 units (topics for all texts, talks and tasks)
1. My World: people we admire, family and friends, personal experiences, achievements, hopes and dreams, hobbies.
2. Sport and Healthy Living: sports, sports stars (invented ones only), healthy food and drink, getting ready for exercise, teamwork, competitions.
3. Living Things: animals and their special features, how animals survive, habitats, food chains, plants and how they live, looking after pets.
4. Inventions and Technology: useful inventions, how inventions changed travel and communication, gadgets, machines, using technology well.

## Use of English objectives tested (Stage 6)
6Ug.01 questions to develop ideas | 6Ug.03 present simple active + passive (is made, are grown) | 6Ug.04 past simple active + passive (was invented, were built) | 6Ug.07 present perfect (recent, indefinite, unfinished past; just, already, yet, ever, never, for, since) | 6Ug.08 future forms (will, going to, present continuous and present simple for the future) | 6Ug.09 first conditional (If it rains, we will...) | 6Ug.10 prepositional / phrasal verbs (look after, look for, find out, give up, take part in, set off, deal with, count on, pick up, run out of) | 6Ug.12 modals (needn't, should, ought to, must, mustn't, have to, don't have to) | 6Us.05 relative clauses (who, which, that, whose, where, whom; non-defining with commas) | 6Us.07 verb + object + infinitive (asked us to bring, want you to try) and give/take/send/bring/show + two objects (showed us the map) | 6Uv.02 prepositions before nouns (on time, in time, by bus, on foot, at the weekend, in danger, without permission, on purpose, by mistake, in fact) | 6Uv.06 collective nouns (a team of players, a flock of birds, a herd of elephants, a swarm of bees, a school of fish, a crowd of people, a pack of wolves, a bunch of flowers, a group of students, staff, audience, class).

## Paper 1: Listening (25 minutes, 19 marks in our practice version)
Every recording is played TWICE. Instructions are short.
- Part 1 (7 questions): a short talk (under 30 seconds, usually two speakers). A question is printed (e.g. "What time does ...?", "Where did ...?", "Which ... did she buy?", "Why was ... ?"). Three PICTURES, A B C. All three pictures are mentioned or suggested in the talk; only one answers the question. Typical traps: a speaker changes their mind ("I was going to... but in the end..."), a second person corrects the first, a time or price that is mentioned but is not the answer, things that "usually" happen versus what happened "this time". Topics: prices, times, days, places, activities, objects, ways of travelling, weather, reasons.
- Part 2 (5 questions): ONE longer talk by one speaker (a teacher, coach, guide, club leader), 80 to 120 seconds, giving information about an event. A notes form with a title and 5 labelled gaps (one example line already filled in). Each answer is one or two words or a number (a time, a number, a day, an object, a type of clothing, a person's surname). One gap is a surname that the speaker SPELLS letter by letter, and spelling must be exact. Traps: two numbers mentioned close together, a first plan that changes.
- Part 3 (7 questions): one conversation, 80 to 120 seconds, between two young people (or a young person and an adult) about an experience, a plan, a game, a hobby or a choice. Questions in the order of the talk, each with three short written options A B C. Questions ask about where/when/how they found out, opinions and reasons ("X likes it because..."), who, how long, which part they like best. The wrong options are all mentioned in the talk but are not the answer to the question.

## Paper 2: Reading and Use of English (35 minutes, 34 marks, 6 parts)
- Part 1 (5 questions): five separate sentences that tell a short connected story or describe one class project/event. Each sentence has one gap and three options printed under it (circle one). Tests verb form and grammar in context: passive vs active, present perfect vs past, modal choice (needn't / mustn't / should), past negative, first conditional (will / would / have). Stem 10 to 25 words.
- Part 2 (7 questions): seven short real-life texts: a sign, a notice, a text message, a note, an email extract, a poster, an app message (15 to 45 words each). Question "What does it say?" or a specific question ("What must students do if...?", "What is X worried about?", "What is X's opinion of...?"). Three options A B C that paraphrase the text; the wrong options use words from the text but change the meaning (must vs can, before vs after, already vs not yet, all vs some).
- Part 3 (5 questions): a conversation between two friends. One friend's lines are given; the other friend's 5 lines are missing. A list of 8 options A to H (one is used in the example, two are extra). Students write the letter. To choose, students must check the line BEFORE and the line AFTER the gap (a question needs an answer; "No problem. What about tomorrow?" follows a refusal; a pronoun must match).
- Part 4 (7 questions): an informal email or letter (100 to 150 words) with 7 gaps plus an example. Write ONE word in each gap. Gaps test grammar words: auxiliaries (have, has, is, was, will), relative pronouns (who, which, whose, where), prepositions (after, for, at, without, of, on), the "to" of an infinitive or of "looking forward to", articles, quantifiers, pronouns.
- Part 5 (5 questions): five short descriptions of young people (30 to 45 words each) saying what they want. Seven texts (40 to 60 words each), e.g. clubs, workshops, books, holidays, websites, trips. Match each person to the ONE text that fits ALL their wishes. Two texts are extra. Each person has 2 or 3 wishes; distractor texts match one wish but not the others.
- Part 6 (5 questions): one longer article written by a young person or about one (200 to 300 words), first-person or third-person, about an experience. Five questions, A B C, in text order: why the person felt something, what surprised them, why someone gave advice, what was best, and a final question on the WHOLE text: "What is the writer mainly doing in this article?" (purpose / main idea).

## Paper 3: Writing (35 minutes, 25 marks)
- Task 1 (10 marks): a short text of 35 to 45 words, usually giving a friend instructions or advice. Three bullet points to cover (prepare / steps / why one step matters). Marked for: content (all 3 bullets, 4 marks), organisation (clear order, connectives, 3 marks), language (vocabulary, grammar incl. advice forms should / must / don't forget to, spelling, 3 marks).
- Task 2 (15 marks): a text of 90 to 110 words describing something or giving an opinion, with three bullet points and the instruction "Give your writing a suitable title". Marked for: content (all 3 bullets with detail, 4), communicative achievement (clear for the reader, 4), organisation (title, paragraphs, linking, 3), language (range and accuracy, 4).

## Item-writing rules
1. Three options in every multiple-choice question. One is clearly right. The other two must be tempting but clearly wrong for a careful reader or listener.
2. Options should be about the same length. Do not make the right answer the longest.
3. Every wrong option gets a short, kind "why" (one sentence) that tells the child what to check.
4. Write the "worked" line as a teacher would explain the answer in one or two short sentences, quoting the key words of the text or talk.
5. Hints point at the strategy, never give the answer ("Listen for the time she says AFTER 'but'.").
6. Spread the right answer position (A, B, C) evenly.
7. No trick questions, no answer that depends on world knowledge outside the text.
8. Never copy sentences from any real exam paper or published book.
